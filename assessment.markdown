# The Impact Data Audit

**An independent assessment of whether your data can prove what your programs actually do.**

## The question

When a funder asks *"what outcomes did our grant produce?"* — can you answer from
your systems, or do you assemble the answer by hand?

Most organizations between $5M and $50M don't know which one they are until they
look. You're large enough to carry serious reporting obligations and small enough
that no one owns the data end to end. The honest answer is usually somewhere in
between, and the interesting part is *where*.

That's what the assessment establishes. Not a diagnosis I bring with me — a finding
I reach after looking.

## What I assess

Over two weeks I trace your data from program delivery through to funder report, and
test it against five criteria:

1. **Provenance** — for each number you report to funders, can it be traced back to
   a source record? Which numbers can't?
2. **Reproducibility** — if two staff members produced the same report
   independently, would they agree? Where do the discrepancies come from?
3. **Cost to produce** — actual staff-hours consumed assembling grant reports, board
   packets and compliance filings.
4. **Coverage** — what your programs do versus what your data records. The gap
   between them is what you currently cannot prove, regardless of how good the
   programs are.
5. **Continuity** — what happens to your reporting if the person who knows the
   spreadsheet leaves.

## What you receive

A written assessment, findings first: each criterion rated, each material weakness
stated plainly, with the evidence behind it. Where I find nothing wrong, I say so —
a clean finding is a real result, and worth having in writing before your next
funder conversation.

The report separates what's broken from what's merely inelegant, and flags which
weaknesses would actually survive scrutiny if a major funder examined them.

Your cost: two or three conversations with the people who touch the data, and
read-only access. No system changes, no software, no commitment.

## Independence and disclosure

You should know my interest before you start, not after.

I do build data systems, and some assessments surface problems I'd be a reasonable
person to fix. Others don't. I write the findings before I know which kind it is,
and I'll hand you a report that recommends doing nothing if that's what I find —
that outcome is common enough that I've stopped treating it as a failed engagement.

If you'd rather remove the question entirely, take the report to someone else to
implement. It's written to be usable that way.
